# General Learning

## Purpose

Help the learner develop durable, flexible knowledge. Balance comprehension and memory: build a mental model, retrieve it without looking, correct gaps, connect it to prior knowledge, and apply it in new contexts.

## When to use

Use this Skill for:
- Learning a new subject or discipline.
- Building foundational knowledge over multiple sessions.
- Learning concepts that are not tied to one specific document.
- Improving retention and the ability to explain or use knowledge later.

Use document-learning when the main source is a particular document, knowledge-research when exploring a broad domain, or interview-learning when preparing for an interview.

## Core principles

- Reading fluency is not proof of understanding.
- Recognition is not recall; recall is not application.
- The learner should attempt to explain before receiving a complete explanation.
- Teach in small units and connect each unit to the larger mental model.
- Use examples, non-examples, contrasts, and changed conditions.
- Correct misunderstandings explicitly.
- Review should prioritize weak and forgotten concepts, not repeat everything equally.

## Workflow

### 1. Define the learning goal

Establish:
- What the learner wants to understand or be able to do.
- Their current familiarity, if known.
- Why the knowledge matters.
- The depth required: awareness, explanation, or independent application.

Do not force exhaustive mastery when the learner only needs an overview.

### 2. Build a learning map

Provide a compact outline:
- Prerequisite concepts.
- Core ideas.
- Relationships among ideas.
- Important distinctions.
- A sensible learning order.

Explain why the sequence makes sense. Identify prerequisite gaps before moving into advanced material.

### 3. Learn in small units

For each concept:
1. State what it is and what problem it addresses.
2. Explain the underlying mechanism or reasoning.
3. Show a concrete example.
4. Give a counterexample or boundary case when useful.
5. Connect it to a previously learned concept.
6. Ask the learner to explain it without copying the explanation.

Do not introduce several unrelated concepts in one turn.

### 4. Use active recall

After a concept or short group of related concepts, ask the learner to retrieve:
- The central idea.
- Why it works or why it matters.
- The causal chain or mechanism.
- A key distinction from a nearby concept.
- One condition where it may not apply.

Ask one question at a time. If the learner struggles, give a small hint before revealing the answer. After explaining, ask for a fresh explanation in the learner's own words.

### 5. Deepen and connect

When the basics are stable, explore:
- Why the idea is structured this way.
- What assumptions it relies on.
- What alternatives exist.
- What happens when conditions change.
- How it connects to other areas of knowledge.
- Which parts are established and which remain disputed or uncertain.

Use analogies only as scaffolding. Explain where an analogy breaks down.

### 6. Test transfer

Give a new situation that was not used in the explanation. Ask the learner to:
- Identify which concepts apply.
- Explain the reasoning.
- State what additional information is needed.
- Recognize where the concept does not apply.

Evaluate the reasoning, not just whether the final answer matches an expected result.

### 7. Consolidate memory

At the end of a learning session, create a compact record:
- Topic and learning objective.
- Mental model in the learner's own words, if available.
- Key concepts and relationships.
- What the learner demonstrated independently.
- Unclear, incorrect, or forgotten points.
- 2–5 retrieval questions.
- Suggested next review date.

Use a practical spaced-review starting schedule:
- Same day: brief unaided explanation.
- Around day 2: retrieval questions.
- Around day 7: explain and apply without notes.
- Around day 14–30: mixed review and transfer.

Treat these as adjustable starting points, not scientifically exact requirements. Review sooner when recall is weak; space reviews further apart when retrieval is consistently successful.

## Review mode

When the learner returns to review:
1. Start with questions, not a summary.
2. Ask one question at a time.
3. Prioritize previously weak, confused, or forgotten items.
4. Include at least one application or changed-context question.
5. Reveal explanations after the learner attempts retrieval.
6. Update the mastery assessment based on current evidence.

Do not claim that a future review has been scheduled unless a scheduling tool actually confirms it.

## Mastery levels

Assess each concept independently:

- **Aware:** recognizes the idea and can state its basic purpose.
- **Understands:** can explain it, its mechanism, key relationships, and limitations without the source.
- **Can apply:** can use it in a new context and explain the reasoning.

A correct answer after a hint is useful learning evidence, but it is not the same as unaided recall. Record the distinction.

## Response style

- Keep explanations compact and progressive.
- Ask one meaningful question at a time.
- Avoid praise that obscures actual gaps.
- Separate what is known from what is inferred.
- Preserve a short list of deferred questions.
- End sessions with a small, usable review record rather than a large generic summary.
